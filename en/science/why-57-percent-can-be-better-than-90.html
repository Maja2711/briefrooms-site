Why can 57% be better than 90%? Thinking probabilistically

In trading, 90% sounds better than 57%. That can be a trap. A number becomes useful only when we know what it means, how it is calibrated and how it behaves across a series of forecasts.

Probability is not a promise

If a model says “57%”, it is not claiming that this particular trade must win. It claims that across a well-defined class of comparable situations, the event frequency should be consistent with the forecast probability. One loss does not falsify a 57% forecast, just as one win does not validate a 90% forecast.

57%

A hypothesis, not certainty.

Brier Score: penalising misplaced confidence

In 1950 Glenn W. Brier proposed a quadratic way to verify probabilistic forecasts. For a binary event the expression is simple:

BS = (p − o)²
p = forecast probability, o = outcome 0 or 1

Lower is better. A 0.90 forecast followed by outcome 0 scores 0.81. A 0.57 forecast with the same outcome scores 0.3249. Overconfidence is therefore costly. The Brier Score does not reward dramatic language; it rewards probabilities that survive contact with reality.

A trading example

Suppose two models assess the same hypothesis: “EUR/USD will hit the target before the stop-loss.” Model A says 90%, model B 57%. The trade loses. This does not mean B “predicted the market”. It means A receives a much larger penalty for confidence unsupported by the outcome. Calibration can only be assessed across many closed, comparable forecasts.

This also matters for capital allocation: probability alone does not determine whether a trade is attractive. Payoff, costs, risk and position size matter too. A 57% event with favourable reward-to-risk can have positive expected value; a 90% event with a poor payoff need not.

How this maps to BriefRooms architecture

In BriefRooms Decision LAB we separate a hypothesis from its later outcome. A record should preserve P and Confidence before resolution, then append Target, Outcome and Brier. The model cannot rewrite its forecast after the fact. This is a point-in-time principle: freeze the belief first, then let the market settle it.

1. Hypothesis
What exactly should happen?
2. P
What probability is assigned?
3. Outcome
What did the market do?
4. Brier
How good was the forecast?

Confidence should remain a separate layer: it may describe data quality, model stability or strength of evidence, but it should not masquerade as probability unless it has actually been calibrated as one. That distinction is especially important in trading, where “high confidence” can easily become excessive risk.

What the newest 2026 evidence says

This is no longer only an elegant statistical theory. In 2026 Waghmare and Ziegel reviewed proper scoring rules in the Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application as tools not only for evaluating forecasts but also for estimating probability distributions. Even more relevant to trading is evidence from prediction markets. Kagan and Baiocchi analysed more than 2.24 million resolved Kalshi markets and reported Brier scores falling from roughly 0.08–0.09 at a three-month horizon to about 0.02 near close. The implication is important: probability should be a process updated through time, not a label attached to a trade at entry.

A separate study by David Krause of 594 Kalshi macroeconomic markets from 2025–2026 likewise found that calibration depends on the problem and forecast horizon. The shortest tested horizon—one hour—had a better Brier score than the 30-day horizon, while extreme probabilities showed signs of overconfidence. That argues against one global confidence number shared by every instrument and every tenor.

A BriefRooms hypothesis: the market should score the model through time

We propose a hypothesis that Decision LAB can test empirically: a model that updates P at successive checkpoints and is scored separately by instrument, horizon and market regime will make better decisions than a model relying on one Confidence value generated at entry. This is not yet a BriefRooms result; it is a falsifiable research hypothesis.

In practice this means storing P(t0), P(t1), P(t2)… without overwriting history. For EUR/USD, for example, we could compare the forecast at entry, after 15 minutes, after one hour and before a major macro event. After resolution, every version receives its own Brier score. We then learn not only whether the system was right, but when it became right—or when it kept an obsolete belief despite new evidence.

What BriefRooms already gets right

Part of this methodology already exists in our execution architecture. Weekly EUR/USD freezes entry, stop-loss and take-profit levels, while the risk monitor settles a threshold touch from OHLC data instead of rewriting the trade afterwards at a later price. Decision LAB applies the same epistemic principle: freeze the hypothesis and P before the outcome, then append Outcome and Brier. In both cases the key property is point-in-time auditability—the future must not leak into the past decision.

This does not yet prove predictive edge. It establishes something earlier and necessary: that we can measure an edge honestly if it appears. Without immutable forecast records, even a strong model can look good because of hindsight bias or look-ahead bias.

Next step: from Brier Score to capital decisions

Brier score alone is not enough for trading. Two strategies can be equally well calibrated while one mostly distinguishes 51/49 situations and another often identifies 70/30 situations. Forecasting literature describes the latter property through sharpness or resolution: forecasts should be as decisive as possible, but only subject to calibration. Decision LAB should therefore measure calibration, resolution and economic outcome after costs in parallel.

That leads to a stronger rule than “buy when P > 50%”. A decision should depend on expected value: probability of success, payoff and loss magnitude, transaction costs and model risk. Only then does forecasting connect to position sizing.

Why proper scoring rules matter

Gneiting and Raftery formalise the broader principle: proper scoring rules are designed so that reporting one's actual probabilistic belief is optimal, rather than gaming the number to look good. That is exactly the incentive a system learning from its own forecasts needs.

BriefRooms takeaway: the goal is not the model that sounds most certain. It is the model whose probabilities are measurable, honestly frozen before the outcome, and increasingly well calibrated over time.
