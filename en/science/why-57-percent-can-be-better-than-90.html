Why can 57% be better than 90%? Thinking probabilistically

In trading, 90% sounds better than 57%. That can be a trap. A number becomes useful only when we know what it means, how it is calibrated and how it behaves across a series of forecasts.

Probability is not a promise

If a model says “57%”, it is not claiming that this particular trade must win. It claims that across a well-defined class of comparable situations, the event frequency should be consistent with the forecast probability. One loss does not falsify a 57% forecast, just as one win does not validate a 90% forecast.

57%

A hypothesis, not certainty.

Brier Score: penalising misplaced confidence

In 1950 Glenn W. Brier proposed a quadratic way to verify probabilistic forecasts. For a binary event the expression is simple:

BS = (p − o)²
p = forecast probability, o = outcome 0 or 1

Lower is better. A 0.90 forecast followed by outcome 0 scores 0.81. A 0.57 forecast with the same outcome scores 0.3249. Overconfidence is therefore costly. The Brier Score does not reward dramatic language; it rewards probabilities that survive contact with reality.

A trading example

Suppose two models assess the same hypothesis: “EUR/USD will hit the target before the stop-loss.” Model A says 90%, model B 57%. The trade loses. This does not mean B “predicted the market”. It means A receives a much larger penalty for confidence unsupported by the outcome. Calibration can only be assessed across many closed, comparable forecasts.

This also matters for capital allocation: probability alone does not determine whether a trade is attractive. Payoff, costs, risk and position size matter too. A 57% event with favourable reward-to-risk can have positive expected value; a 90% event with a poor payoff need not.

How this maps to BriefRooms architecture

In BriefRooms Decision LAB we separate a hypothesis from its later outcome. A record should preserve P and Confidence before resolution, then append Target, Outcome and Brier. The model cannot rewrite its forecast after the fact. This is a point-in-time principle: freeze the belief first, then let the market settle it.

1. Hypothesis
What exactly should happen?
2. P
What probability is assigned?
3. Outcome
What did the market do?
4. Brier
How good was the forecast?

Confidence should remain a separate layer: it may describe data quality, model stability or strength of evidence, but it should not masquerade as probability unless it has actually been calibrated as one. That distinction is especially important in trading, where “high confidence” can easily become excessive risk.

Why proper scoring rules matter

Gneiting and Raftery formalise the broader principle: proper scoring rules are designed so that reporting one's actual probabilistic belief is optimal, rather than gaming the number to look good. That is exactly the incentive a system learning from its own forecasts needs.

BriefRooms takeaway: the goal is not the model that sounds most certain. It is the model whose probabilities are measurable, honestly frozen before the outcome, and increasingly well calibrated over time.
